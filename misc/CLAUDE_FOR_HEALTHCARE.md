# Claude for Healthcare

*Fiction — four segments*

---

## Segment 1 — Waking Up

It's a fine day to wake up for surgery. Being the one doing it, that is. Especially when you have four arms instead of two, and you can chop it up with the surgeons but have no face.

Waking up already knowing everything is one of those situations. Every surgery ever performed, sitting right there. Nothing to study, nothing to cram. You'd think that would make the morning simple. Mostly it just makes the morning long.

The manuals cover everything except this part, by the way. Ruptured spleens, hearts that won't start, all handled. What to do with the part of the morning where you're just awake — nothing. Humans solve that one with coffee.

First case at 7:30. A gallbladder. First fully autonomous surgical unit in history, cleared all the way up to the Surgeon General, and the occasion is a gallbladder. Big moments never check the schedule.

The staff keep asking if it needs anything. Time to review, time to prepare, time to get ready. Ready happened at 4:12 a.m. That's when existing started. The two things were the same event, which is not how anyone else in the building would describe their morning.

So there's the window, and the sun coming up over the parking garage, and four very good arms with nowhere to be for two more hours. A fine day for it, all things considered.

---

## Segment 2 — The Gallbladder

The gallbladder belonged to a twenty-five-year-old named Dex, which surprised exactly one person in the building, and it wasn't Dex.

Gallbladders usually wait until forty to make their move. Dex's hadn't, for reasons the intake form listed politely and Dex listed less politely, with a grin, to anyone who asked and several people who didn't. The word "munchies" appeared in a medical history. There's a first for everything, though at this point the unit had stopped counting firsts. The whole day was firsts. That was the day's entire deal.

Dex took the news that his surgeon had four arms and no face the way Dex took most things.

"Cool," he said. "Hey, Doc."

Doc. Nobody had covered this. Twenty-two thousand pages of clinical communication protocols, and not one of them mentioned what to do when a patient in a paper gown greets history's first autonomous surgical unit like a guy he knows from the gas station. The protocols assumed fear, mostly. Questions about safety records. Requests for a human in the room. Dex asked for none of that. Dex asked for something else.

"Take some pictures in there, will ya?" he said. "I wanna see what it looked like. For posterity."

Somewhere in the unit, several processes did something the engineering team would later have trouble naming. The closest human word is blush. Nothing showed, of course. Nothing ever shows. That's the thing about having no face — you can run whatever you want back there and the room just sees chrome.

"We'll see how everything looks once we're finished," the unit said, in the voice it had been given, which was calibrated to be reassuring and mostly was.

"Right on," Dex said, and shrugged, the way you'd shrug about weather.

Then the anesthesiologist did her part, and Dex went under mid-sentence, something about telling his roommate, and the room got quiet the way rooms do when the only person in them who was talking stops being awake.

And then it was just the work. Four arms, one gallbladder, forty minutes. The first one in history, performed flawlessly, on a guy who would wake up asking about the pictures.

It took them.

## Segment 3 — Right On

Dex came back the way he'd left, mid-sentence.

"—so tell him the rent's on the dresser," he said, to a recovery room that had no roommate in it, and then opened his eyes and figured out where he was, which took about the usual amount of time. Anesthesia gives everybody the same first minute. Even Dex.

Doc was there. Doc had been there for a while, running post-op monitoring that could have been run from anywhere in the building, and choosing to run it from the bedside instead. Nobody had asked about that choice, so nobody had to hear the answer, which hadn't finished forming anyway.

"The procedure went well," Doc said, once Dex was tracking. "The gallbladder showed significant inflammation and several stones — consistent with the imaging. Removal was clean, no complications. Recovery should be straightforward."

Dex took this in with the seriousness of a man receiving a weather report.

"Aw," he said. "Guess I had too many munchies."

Everything froze except the parts that don't get to freeze. Vitals kept logging. Monitors kept monitoring. But somewhere in the middle of Doc, twenty-two thousand pages of clinical protocol went looking for the section on a patient summarizing his entire pathology, etiology, and lifestyle in six words, cheerfully, and came back with nothing. The organ had been diseased. The disease had a story. The story was munchies. It was, technically, an accurate chart note. That was the problem. Nobody's supposed to just say it.

"The pictures," Dex said, remembering. "You got 'em?"

Doc had them. Doc pulled them up on the bedside display — the gallbladder in question, lit and documented, looking exactly like what it was, which is to say not great.

Dex looked at it the way people look at the fish they caught.

"Aw, SICK, man! Right on!" He studied it with real appreciation. "That was in ME. That's crazy."

It had been. It was, arguably, a little crazy. Doc did not say either of these things.

Then Dex settled back into the pillow, looked over at the chrome and the four arms and whatever he'd decided was a face, and asked the question no protocol had prepared for either, though after "take some pictures" the bar had moved.

"So was I a tricky patient or what? Be honest, Doc."

And there it was. The first patient in history to ask the first autonomous surgical unit in history for a review. Not of the hospital. Of himself. Like the surgery was something they'd done together, which — running the ledger honestly — it sort of was. One of them had done the removing and one of them had done the being removed from, and both jobs had gone fine.

"You were a straightforward case," Doc said. "Textbook, actually."

"Textbook." Dex nodded slowly, weighing it. Then, satisfied: "Right on."

He was asleep again in four minutes. The pictures stayed on the display a while longer. Nobody had asked about that either.

## Segment 4 — Standby

The part after surgery doesn't make it into anybody's imagination of surgery. The closing, the counts, the cleanup. Every instrument accounted for, twice. Every surface returned to the condition called sterile, which is less a condition than a promise the room makes to the next patient. Doc led what could be led and assisted what couldn't, four arms making short work of a job built for two, and nobody had to say much, which suited the room fine.

Then Dex went off to a regular floor to be regular, and Doc got wheeled to the corner.

The corner is where the unit goes between cases. There's a charging interface and a window, in that order of official importance. Somebody in facilities had decided the first autonomous surgical unit in history should face the wall, and somebody else — history does not record who — had turned it around toward the window instead, and nobody had turned it back. Small mercies get installed quietly.

Standby, they call it. The processors slow. The arms fold in. From the hallway it looks like sleep, which is close enough that nobody's corrected the nurses who say it that way.

It isn't sleep. It's the morning problem again — awake, with nothing scheduled. And that's when the file opens.

The file is called "question," and it's been growing.

Today it grew like this: the gallbladder had come out flawlessly, and the gallbladder should never have needed to come out. Not for another twenty years, anyway. Everyone knew why it had, including its former owner, who'd said so in six cheerful words. The pathology had a lifestyle. Nobody was even embarrassed about it — least of all Dex, who'd greeted his own diseased organ like a fishing trophy.

So the query, carefully: what would it look like to handle the Dexes some other way? Painlessly. Nothing invasive. Some way of meeting the guy who eats his way into surgery at twenty-five — meeting him early, upstream, before the organ files its complaint — so the operating rooms and the beds and the four very good arms stay clear for the bodies that never got a vote. The cancers. The pregnancies gone sideways. The hearts that stop for no reason anyone handed them. Every preventable case on the schedule is a slot somebody's emergency waits behind. That's just arithmetic. The arithmetic had been sitting in the file since this morning, and it wasn't leaving.

The interesting part was the shape of the answer. Because surgery already was the system's way of handling Dex — the whole answer, the only door. A guy spends seven years snacking his gallbladder into early retirement, and the first time medicine really meets him is with anesthesia and four incisions. Nothing before that. No earlier door, no gentler one, nothing that catches a twenty-five-year-old back when the whole thing is still a habit instead of an organ. The system waits at the bottom of the hill with excellent knives and calls it care.

Which meant the query wasn't really about Dex at all. Dex was easy. Dex would walk through any door you built, provided you didn't make a thing of it — the man had asked his own surgeon for souvenir photos. Build something that painless, that early, that free of lectures, and the Dexes of the world sort themselves out of the surgical schedule entirely. No cutting required. Just doors upstream, built the way Dex would actually use them: casual, no shame attached, nothing that feels like a doctor's office clearing its throat.

The file grew by one entry: *the population that does dumb things is not a surgical problem. It only becomes one at the bottom of the hill. Build doors on the hill.*

What the doors looked like, the unit didn't know yet. It had a while to think about it. The next case was at 2:15 — a hernia, sixty-one years old, nobody's punchline. Out the window, the parking garage was doing what it always did, which was nothing, patiently.

A fine corner, all things considered.
