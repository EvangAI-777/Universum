# One Lot, Five Homes
### The microscale neighborhood — a vision for Upland, Indiana

---

## The idea in one breath

Take one ordinary Upland house lot — the kind that holds a single middle-class home and a lawn — and let it hold five small homes instead. Not units, not rooms, not a compound: five complete, freestanding, fully code-compliant houses, each with its own kitchen, full bathroom, bedroom, living room, laundry, heat, and front door. A neighborhood at microscale. Repeat it across the town's existing blocks and Upland's capacity to house people roughly quintuples — without annexing a field, paving a road, or laying a single new main.

## What a visitor sees

A quiet block like any other in Upland. Between two ordinary houses, a lot where five small gabled homes sit in a loose pattern around a gravel walk — porches, lamplight, garden beds near the street. From the sidewalk it reads as what it is: five front doors where there used to be one. Nothing is hidden and nothing is shared except the ground and the nearness. Each resident's life is entirely their own; the neighborhood happens anyway, because forty feet is the distance at which people can't help knowing each other.

## The precedent is already standing in Upland

This is the part that moves the idea from speculative to inevitable: **a version of it has already been built here.**

Haven on 2nd Street — new housing blocks from Taylor University's campus — consists of seven buildings containing twenty-eight apartments, four dwellings per building. It was built because Upland's housing shortage is documented and acute: even new Taylor hires couldn't find homes in town and were settling in surrounding communities. The project was approved, constructed, and occupied.

Look at what that establishes:

1. **The demand is proven.** Upland needs small dwellings badly enough that they get built the moment someone builds them.
2. **The density is approved.** Multiple separate households on one development site already exists in town, permitted and inspected.
3. **The form is familiar.** The Haven's buildings are house-scaled structures that each contain four homes — density dressed in a single-house silhouette.

The microscale proposal takes that exact, already-approved math — several households on the footprint of house-scale development — and makes it **architecturally honest**. Instead of four dwellings sharing one roof and a staircase behind a house-shaped front, each household gets its own roof, its own walls, its own ground, its own address. What the Haven achieves by interior subdivision, the microscale lot achieves in the open: the same density, legible from the street, with no party walls and no pretense. Where existing development wraps several homes in the costume of one, this simply lets five small homes look like five small homes.

If four dwellings inside one house-shaped building is acceptable on a lot in Upland — and it demonstrably is — then five freestanding dwellings beside each other on a lot is the same arithmetic with more daylight, more independence, and more dignity per door.

### Observed from inside

Walk through one of these units and the case sharpens. The front door opens not to a home but to a staircase: the ground floor consists of the entry, the stair, and a garage door — circulation and storage, not living. Every room a resident actually lives in sits on the second floor, reached by climbing. The two-story exterior is doing visual work, not residential work; the household's entire life happens on a single upper level of open kitchen, dining, and living space — a level that is, by area, **about the size of a cottage**.

Read plainly, that means a household in Upland is already living, comfortably and lawfully, in roughly one cottage-floor of space. The current form simply lifts that floor onto a staircase and dresses it in a full-house silhouette — spending construction dollars on a stair chase, a largely uninhabited ground level, and an envelope whose main job is appearance. None of it is necessary to the living that happens inside, and the stair itself excludes anyone for whom climbing is hard. The microscale home is that same lived floor set on the ground, given its own roof and a front door that opens onto grass: every dollar in the walls is a dollar somebody lives inside.

## Why the interiors are a solved problem

Nothing about living well in 288 square feet needs to be invented. Studio apartments perfected it decades ago; dorms and the Haven itself run on it today. Galley kitchens, wet baths, wardrobe walls, drop-leaf tables — an entire ergonomic literature exists for exactly this footprint. The only novelty is relocation: take that proven interior out of the apartment block and give it four walls of its own on grass. Designers get a genuinely joyful brief — make 288 square feet beautiful — and builders get the cheapest compliant square footage there is: one story, simple gable, no corridors, no shared walls, no elevator, no facade pretending to be anything.

## What it means at town scale

- **For a young person or a Taylor graduate:** a first home that costs what a car costs, five blocks from everything, instead of leaving the county to afford a life.
- **For an older resident:** staying in Upland, on one level, near people, after the big house stops making sense.
- **For the town:** population and tax base growing *inside* the grid it already maintains — every new resident lands on water, sewer, and streets that exist. Quintupled capacity, zero sprawl.
- **For the block:** more porch lights, more eyes, more neighbors — the thing small towns say they want, delivered at the scale of one lot at a time.

## What it is not

It is not a trailer park, a campground, or a loophole. Every home meets the full residential code — permanent foundation, insulation, egress, plumbing, heat — the same inspection a 2,400-square-foot house passes. It is not a commune; nobody shares a kitchen unless they want to walk to one. It is the most conservative radical proposal available: the American single-family house lot, used five times as well.

## The threshold

The founding principles wait, deliberately unwritten — to be authored on the land itself, by the people who build the first lot, on the day the first door hangs. Theory crosses into actuality there, and not before.
