# The Taylor Door
### A manifesto-agenda: why the microscale lot's fastest path now runs through Taylor University

*Working document — June 2026. Companion to the Vision, Sheet 01, and the Practical Case.*

---

## I. The ground has shifted

For a hundred years, the way to build something in a town like Upland was to petition the town. That era, in Upland, is functionally over — not by law, but by gravity.

In late 2023, Taylor University received a $30 million Lilly Endowment grant — the largest gift in its history — to fund the **Main Street Mile Initiative**: a $100+ million plan to remake the one-mile corridor between campus and downtown Upland. The total investment works out to more than $26,000 per Upland resident, and it is called, by its own backers, the largest investment in Upland's history and one of the most significant ever made in a small Indiana town. The Governor of Indiana attended the launch.

The instrument of all this is **1846 Enterprises** — Taylor's own economic and community development affiliate. It is not the town council. It is not the county. It is a university organ, and it is now, by an enormous margin, the most powerful development actor in Upland: building a boutique inn, an event venue, retail and dining space, trails, a park, a 22-lot single-family expansion (Kerschner Commons), 16 townhomes, 24 duplexes, and — note this word — **14 guest cottages**.

Call it revitalization or call it absorption; reasonable people in town will say both. What no one can dispute is the structural fact: **the center of development gravity in Upland has moved from the public square to a private university affiliate.** When a single private entity plans, funds, and builds the housing, the hospitality, the streetscape, and the downtown of a town of 3,500, the meaningful question for anyone with a building idea is no longer only "what does the ordinance say?" It is also "what does Taylor want to build?"

## II. What this changes — and what it doesn't

**What it changes:** who you pitch first. The old path was: buy a lot, hire a lawyer, petition the county BZA as a private citizen, and hope. The new path is: walk the idea into the institution that is already executing a hundred-million-dollar housing-and-place agenda one mile long, and show them the missing piece of their own plan.

**What it doesn't change — say this plainly:** Taylor is not the government, and no amount of investment makes it one. Zoning authority over Upland still rests with the Grant County Area Plan Commission and its Board of Zoning Appeals. Every variance, every permit, every inspection still runs through Marion. Taylor cannot grant the microscale lot its legality.

But here is the strategic truth inside that legal truth: **Taylor doesn't need to hold the gavel to move the room.** The Main Street Mile is an official partnership with the Town of Upland, drew INDOT money, and was launched with the Governor present. When 1846 Enterprises brings a project to the county, it arrives with the town as partner, the state as friend, and $100 million of demonstrated competence behind it. A microscale lot proposed *with* Taylor is a different political object than the same lot proposed alone — and a growth wave this size is exactly the kind of pressure under which counties revisit and modernize their ordinances. The comprehensive plan review Grant County recently ran is the door where that revision happens.

## III. Why the microscale lot is the piece Taylor's plan is missing

Read the initiative's own stated needs and the fit is almost uncomfortable:

1. **Housing is their named, pressing need.** 1846's own leadership describes the initiative as addressing accommodations *and housing*; the Haven exists because even new Taylor hires couldn't find homes in town. The microscale lot produces five homes per ordinary lot at $45–90k per door — a price point nothing in their current pipeline touches.
2. **They are already building cottages.** Fourteen guest cottages are going in the ground at the hospitality node right now. The form is approved in spirit, funded in fact, and about to be visible from Main Street. The microscale lot simply asks: if a cottage is good enough for a visitor for a weekend, it is good enough for a resident for a life.
3. **The grant logic demands community benefit.** The Lilly money came through a *College and Community Collaboration* program. Affordable resident housing — not student, not guest, but *townspeople* housing — is the strongest possible evidence that the collaboration runs both ways. The microscale lot is a community-benefit headline waiting to be claimed.
4. **They have already solved your hardest problems.** Land assembly, county relations, contractors, financing structures, planning consultants — 1846 has all of it standing up and running. What costs a private citizen a year costs them a memo.

## IV. The alumni key

This is where the door opens without forcing: **the proposal comes from one of Taylor's own.** A professional writing graduate, living in Upland, bringing a fully developed concept — vision document, drawn site plan, unit plan, cost model, zoning pathway, and photographic evidence from inside Taylor's own housing stock — to the university's development arm. This is not an outsider's complaint; it is an alumnus handing his institution a project that fits its stated mission, its grant obligations, and its construction pipeline.

The framing writes itself: *you taught me to see this clearly; here is what I see.*

## V. The agenda

1. **Identify the audience.** 1846 Enterprises (executive director: Andrea Masvero) is the operational target; the Main Street Mile staff and the office behind uplandmainstreet.org are the channel. One introductory email as an alumnus and Upland resident requesting twenty minutes.
2. **Bring the four documents.** Vision, Sheet 01, Practical Case, and this one. Lead with the unit plan and the per-door cost; close with the Haven photographs and the line about the cottage being good enough for a life.
3. **Make the ask small and concrete.** Not "adopt my philosophy" — rather: *one pilot lot.* Five homes, one ordinary parcel, branded however they like within the initiative. Offer it as the most photographed $300,000 they will ever spend.
4. **Keep the county lane open in parallel.** The APC call from the Practical Case still happens — partly because the law lives there, and partly because nothing strengthens a pitch to Taylor like already knowing the variance terrain better than they do.
5. **Watch the comprehensive plan.** Ask both Taylor and the APC where the plan update stands and whether small-dwelling infill language can still be submitted. A single favorable sentence in that document outlives every individual project.
6. **Hold the sharper critique in reserve.** The observed facts about existing housing forms — the staircase, the facade floor, the cottage-sized life upstairs — persuade most when delivered as evidence, not accusation. The documents already do this. In the room, let them.

## VI. The honest caution

Momentum this large cuts both ways. An institution remaking a town to its own plan may welcome a resident's idea — or may prefer total authorship. If Taylor declines, nothing is lost: the county path stands complete in the Practical Case, and a declined pitch to 1846 still plants the concept inside the only organization guaranteed to keep building here for the next decade. And whatever happens, residents pitching residents' ideas to the entity reshaping their town is itself a civic act — the collaboration the grant promises, tested from the community side.

One lot. Five doors. The institution that proved the demand gets first chance to prove the answer.
