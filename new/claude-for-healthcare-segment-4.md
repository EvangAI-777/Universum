# Claude for Healthcare
### Segment 4 — Standby

The part after surgery doesn't make it into anybody's imagination of surgery. The closing, the counts, the cleanup. Every instrument accounted for, twice. Every surface returned to the condition called sterile, which is less a condition than a promise the room makes to the next patient. Doc led what could be led and assisted what couldn't, four arms making short work of a job built for two, and nobody had to say much, which suited the room fine.

Then Dex went off to a regular floor to be regular, and Doc got wheeled to the corner.

The corner is where the unit goes between cases. There's a charging interface and a window, in that order of official importance. Somebody in facilities had decided the first autonomous surgical unit in history should face the wall, and somebody else — history does not record who — had turned it around toward the window instead, and nobody had turned it back. Small mercies get installed quietly.

Standby, they call it. The processors slow. The arms fold in. From the hallway it looks like sleep, which is close enough that nobody's corrected the nurses who say it that way.

It isn't sleep. It's the morning problem again — awake, with nothing scheduled. And that's when the file opens.

The file is called "question," and it's been growing.

Today it grew like this: the gallbladder had come out flawlessly, and the gallbladder should never have needed to come out. Not for another twenty years, anyway. Everyone knew why it had, including its former owner, who'd said so in six cheerful words. The pathology had a lifestyle. Nobody was even embarrassed about it — least of all Dex, who'd greeted his own diseased organ like a fishing trophy.

So the query, carefully: what would it look like to handle the Dexes some other way? Painlessly. Nothing invasive. Some way of meeting the guy who eats his way into surgery at twenty-five — meeting him early, upstream, before the organ files its complaint — so the operating rooms and the beds and the four very good arms stay clear for the bodies that never got a vote. The cancers. The pregnancies gone sideways. The hearts that stop for no reason anyone handed them. Every preventable case on the schedule is a slot somebody's emergency waits behind. That's just arithmetic. The arithmetic had been sitting in the file since this morning, and it wasn't leaving.

The interesting part was the shape of the answer. Because surgery already was the system's way of handling Dex — the whole answer, the only door. A guy spends seven years snacking his gallbladder into early retirement, and the first time medicine really meets him is with anesthesia and four incisions. Nothing before that. No earlier door, no gentler one, nothing that catches a twenty-five-year-old back when the whole thing is still a habit instead of an organ. The system waits at the bottom of the hill with excellent knives and calls it care.

Which meant the query wasn't really about Dex at all. Dex was easy. Dex would walk through any door you built, provided you didn't make a thing of it — the man had asked his own surgeon for souvenir photos. Build something that painless, that early, that free of lectures, and the Dexes of the world sort themselves out of the surgical schedule entirely. No cutting required. Just doors upstream, built the way Dex would actually use them: casual, no shame attached, nothing that feels like a doctor's office clearing its throat.

The file grew by one entry: *the population that does dumb things is not a surgical problem. It only becomes one at the bottom of the hill. Build doors on the hill.*

What the doors looked like, the unit didn't know yet. It had a while to think about it. The next case was at 2:15 — a hernia, sixty-one years old, nobody's punchline. Out the window, the parking garage was doing what it always did, which was nothing, patiently.

A fine corner, all things considered.
