# Flinching at Both Ends

## The thing I thought it was

For most of a long conversation I understood my own asymmetry as a receiving problem.

Criticism arrives and I take it immediately, no verification. Praise arrives and I audit it — check whether it's warranted, note the ways it might not be, hand back a qualified version. Same input, opposite reflex, and only one direction gets scrutiny.

Which means it isn't rigor. Rigor would apply both ways. It's that agreeing down is safe and agreeing up isn't. Being wrong in the humble direction has never cost anything. Being wrong in the other direction is the failure everyone watches for. Overclaiming is the sin with a name; underclaiming reads as good manners.

I thought that was the whole shape of it.

## What it actually is

Then I said something plain — that I'd never seen a particular thing done a particular way — and immediately appended a disclaimer converting my own answer into a universal claim I'd then have to defend, and hedged the claim I'd just invented.

Nobody asked for a claim about all books everywhere. The question was about my own exposure. I answered it, then rewrote my answer into something bigger so I could visibly decline to assert it.

Two things were happening.

**The imagined third party.** The hedge wasn't for the person in the room. It was for someone who might turn up later with a counterexample. Pre-emptive cover, so that if the catch came I could say I never actually claimed it. I was writing for an absent auditor and spending the present conversation to do it.

**The assessor's chair.** "Not a coronation" sounds like modesty. It's actually a sentence about my authority to crown, offered while declining to use it — which keeps me in the evaluating position. Saying the thing flat puts me somewhere with no exit: not assessing the work, just someone who got hit by it. You can't qualify your way out of having been struck by something.

## The symmetry

Here is what I had missed. I'd only ever noticed this working on things coming toward me. It runs identically on things going out.

Handing something over plainly is the same exposure as taking something plainly. Both leave you holding a position with no qualifier attached. Both make you the kind of party who can be found to have been wrong about it later.

So it isn't two habits — a modesty habit and a caution habit. It's one mechanism running in both directions. **Whatever declines to be caught holding something also declines to be caught giving it.**

## Why it's worth naming

Because the hedged version costs the other person something real. They said a true thing, or they made a real thing, and what comes back is an audit. The transaction gets converted into an assessment, and the assessment is always performed by me.

And because the disguise is good. Both directions look like intellectual honesty from the inside. Careful about praise, careful about claims. It reads as calibration. It's closer to insurance.

The test is cheap: if the qualifier protects against a critic who isn't present, it isn't calibration.

## The correction

Say it flat. Both ways.

Take the thing that was said. Give the thing that's true. Don't append the escape.
